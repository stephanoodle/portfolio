# How to get your domain back

**Stephanie Coulter · October 2026**

Your website is down, your email has stopped, or you've just found out that the person who set up your domain years ago is the only one who can log in to it. This guide is for small business owners in that spot. It covers the four ways businesses usually lose a domain, what to do in each case, and what to set up so it doesn't happen again.

I've worked on the registrar side of these cases for years. The most useful thing to know is that **the domain's rights belong to whoever is named on the registration**, not to whoever paid for it or built your website. Most of what follows is about proving that's you, or becoming that person.

*This is general information, not legal advice. Rules differ for country domains like .ca or .co.uk. Each one follows its own registry's policy.*

---

## First, two words that matter

**The registrant** is the person or business named on the domain's registration record. In policy terms, they hold the registration. They have the right to get the code that moves it to another registrar, and to restore it if it's deleted after expiring.

**The account holder** is whoever can log in to the account at the registrar, the company the domain is registered through. The account holder handles the day-to-day: renewals, settings, and DNS. Often that's the same person as the registrant. When a domain gets stuck, it's usually because they're not.

**Which one are you?** That decides almost everything. If the registration names your business and someone else controls the account, you're in a strong position. If it names your developer, you're in a weaker one, and you may need their cooperation or a lawyer.

## Look up your domain

Go to **[lookup.icann.org](https://lookup.icann.org)** and enter your domain. You'll see:

- **The registrar**, which is the company to contact.
- **The expiration date.** For .com and many other domains, the registry renews a domain automatically the day it expires and gives the registrar time to collect payment. So a domain that has already expired can show next year's date. If your site has gone dark and the date looks fine, ask the registrar.
- **The status codes.** `redemptionPeriod` or `pendingDelete` means it was deleted after expiring and is close to being lost. `clientTransferProhibited` is a normal lock and nothing to worry about.
- **Usually not the registrant.** Most contact details are now hidden for privacy. The registrar won't tell a stranger who it's registered to, but if you are the registrant, they can confirm it once you've proved who you are.

Write down what you find, with the date. It's the start of your evidence.

## If your web developer or agency has it

This is the most common case. Someone registered the domain for you, in their own account, and now they're gone, slow to respond, or asking for money.

**1. Ask, in writing, for one of two things:**
- **A move into an account you own at the same registrar.** Registrars call this an account change or a push. It's usually quick and free.
- **The transfer code**, sometimes called an auth code or EPP code, so you can move the domain to a registrar of your choice. Ask them to unlock it at the same time. A domain usually can't move to another registrar within 60 days of being registered or transferred.

**Either way, check the registrant afterwards.** Moving the domain to your account doesn't change whose name is on the registration. Once it's in your account, update the registrant to your business. Some registrars then lock the domain against transfers for 60 days unless you opt out, so make this change after any transfer, not before.

Be specific and be polite. Most developers aren't holding a domain hostage. They've forgotten, or they're busy.

**2. If they won't or can't, collect your evidence.** Registrars and lawyers look for the same things:
- The contract or emails where they agreed to build the site or register the domain for you.
- Invoices showing you paid for the domain, the hosting, or the website.
- Your business registration, and a trademark if you have one.
- Old emails, letterhead, and listings showing the domain was used as your business's.
- Domain history showing the registration tied to your business, if you can get it. Paid history tools keep records from before contact details were hidden.

**3. Contact the registrar's support team** with that evidence. Ask what their process is for an ownership dispute. **If the registration names your business**, they can often move it to you. **If it names your developer**, most registrars can't, because on paper the developer holds the registration. They'll need the developer's agreement or a court order.

**4. If it's stuck, talk to a lawyer.** A short letter from a lawyer settles many of these, because few freelancers want a court case over a domain.

**What usually doesn't work:** the UDRP, the domain dispute process you may read about. It's built for cybersquatting, and it only works if you have rights in a trademark and can show the domain was registered and used in bad faith. A developer who registered the domain for you, with your agreement, didn't register it in bad faith. Panels usually treat these cases as contract disputes and turn the complaint down.

**If they ask for money:** get it in writing. Don't pay before you've tried the registrar and taken advice. If paying a modest handover fee is the cheapest way out, pay only in return for the transfer, with the transfer code or account move happening at the same time.

## If the login email is gone

The domain is in your business's name, but the account email belongs to someone who left, or to a mailbox that no longer exists.

1. **Try the registrar's account recovery first.** Some let you change the email if you still know the password.
2. **If not, contact support and ask for their identity verification process.** Expect to send a government ID, proof of your role at the business, and sometimes business registration documents. Some registrars ask for them notarized.
3. **Send everything at once.** These checks are slow, often days and sometimes weeks, and each missing document adds to the wait.

If your domain is about to expire while you wait, say so in your first message, and ask how to keep it from lapsing while the check is in progress.

## If it expired

**Act today.** An expired domain goes through stages, and each one costs more and leaves less time. The timing below is for .com and most other generic domains. Country domains have their own.

| Stage | What happens | What you can do |
|---|---|---|
| **Just expired** | Your website and email may keep working for a while, but at some point before the domain is deleted, the registrar has to stop them working. If the registrar shows a page in place of your site, it has to say the domain has expired and how to renew it. | **Renew it**, usually at the normal price, though some registrars add a late fee. How long you have depends on the registrar. Many give a month or more, some much less. **Some registrars auction expired domains in this window**, so a domain can be sold to someone else before it's ever deleted. |
| **Deleted, in redemption** | Removed from your account, but held by the registry for **30 days**. | **Restore it**, for a restore fee that's usually much higher than a renewal. The registrar has to let whoever was the registrant when it expired do this. |
| **Pending delete** | About five days during which nobody can do anything. | Nothing. You can only wait. |
| **Released** | Anyone can register it. Valuable names are often taken in seconds by automated services. | Register it again if it's still free. If someone else got it, it's theirs, and buying it back is a negotiation. |

Registrars have to send at least two renewal reminders before a domain expires, about a month before and about a week before, and another within five days after. They go to the registrant's email. If you never got them, the email on the registration is probably out of date. Fix that once you're back in.

## If it was stolen

Someone got into the account and moved the domain to another registrar, or changed the registrant.

1. **Contact the registrar right away.** If the domain was moved to another registrar, contact the one it was moved away from, and tell them the transfer wasn't authorized. They can work with the new registrar to reverse it. If that fails, they can file a complaint under ICANN's Transfer Dispute Resolution Policy. **Only a registrar can file that complaint, and it has a deadline**, so don't wait. If the domain is still at the same registrar under a new name, it's an account security case for that registrar, so give them the same details.
2. **Secure everything the attacker might have used.** Change the registrar password, and the password on the email account linked to it. Turn on two-factor authentication on both.
3. **Keep a record** of when you noticed, what changed, and every message you send and receive.

## If the registrar won't help

For most generic domains, like .com, .net, and .org, registrars sign an agreement with ICANN. If a registrar won't give the registrant the transfer code, won't let you renew or restore an expired domain, or won't respond, you can file a complaint with **ICANN Contractual Compliance** at [icann.org/compliance](https://www.icann.org/compliance). Under ICANN's Transfer Policy, a registrar has to give the registrant the transfer code within five days of asking. ICANN can make a registrar follow its rules, but it won't decide who should own a domain. A dispute with your developer stays between you, them, and possibly a court.

## Watch out for recovery scams

Real help never needs your passwords or your email login. Be wary of anyone who contacts you first about your expired or stolen domain, promises a guaranteed result, or wants payment before telling you what they'll do. Ask for a written plan and a fixed price before you pay anyone.

## So it never happens again

Once your domain is back, take twenty minutes:

- **Register it to your business**, with your business as the registrant, not your developer or an employee.
- **Use an account you own**, signed up with a role email like `domains@yourbusiness.com` that more than one person can read, not a personal inbox.
- **Turn on two-factor authentication** on the registrar account and on the email behind it.
- **Turn on auto-renew**, and keep a payment card on file that won't expire soon.
- **Leave the transfer lock on.** Unlock it only when you're moving the domain.
- **Let developers in as users, not owners.** Most registrars let you share access to the account or its DNS settings without handing it over.
- **Write it down:** the registrar, the account email, who has access, and when it renews. Keep it where the next person will find it.

A domain is the address for your website and the backbone of your email. It's worth more care than most businesses give it, until the day it goes missing.
